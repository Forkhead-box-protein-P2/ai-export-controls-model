AI export controls · a decision model in four steps

What is a month of AI lead worth?

US controls on AI chips, chipmaking tools and memory keep China's stock of compute far below America's. Published estimates turn that gap into a few extra months of US lead at the AI frontier. Whether those months justify what the controls cost depends on what a month of lead changes. This page follows one policy comparison through four steps and reports the price at which the two policies tie.

Every input is sourced, fitted to a published figure, or a labelled judgment with a range. Read each result as: if these numbers, then this answer.

Beliefs

The game tree

The US moves first. China responds by evading, substituting and reserving its own market, and that sets the compute ratio and the lead. Chance then decides whether there is ever a window in which a lead can be converted into durable dominance, who is clearly ahead when it opens, and whether control of the technology is kept. Each branch shows policy A and policy B side by side.

1

Policy to compute

How far apart do controls hold the two compute stocks?

China wants a fixed share of what the US buys each year. Controls bar some of that, and part of what is barred leaks back through smuggling and offshore data centres. Whatever foreign chips do not serve is a captive market for Chinese chipmakers, and Beijing reserves some of the market for them regardless. One drive term then sets how fast Chinese output grows and how fast each chip catches up: the captive share, less the choke from tool and memory controls. Chip controls push that term up and tool controls push it down.

2

Compute to months of lead

How many months of frontier lead is that gap worth?

IFP estimates that each tenfold change in the compute ratio moves the US frontier lead by 5.4 months when Chinese labs can distil US models, and by 10.8 months when they cannot. The lead under each policy is today's lead moved by that rule. What controls buy is the gap between the two lines.

3

Months to outcomes

What do those months change, if anything is ever decided?

Months matter only if there is a window in which a lead can be converted into something durable. The critical lead says how long a lead that takes, which makes decisiveness a matter of degree. When a window opens the leader is clearly ahead with a probability that rises with the lead, and otherwise the transition is contested. Each case carries its own chance that control of the technology is lost, and that outcome is scored the same whoever was ahead.

4

Outcomes to value

What is that worth, and what does buying it cost?

The stakes put a dollar value on the outcome scale. The standing terms accrue every year whether or not a window ever opens: chip sales forgone, friction with third countries, the ordinary value of being ahead, the open-model cost of holding Chinese labs back, and any change in war risk. War risk rises further in decisive worlds to the extent Beijing comes to share the belief that drives the policy.

    What the model leaves out

    • China never ends up with more compute than open access would give it. Its yearly purchases are capped at its appetite. Faster indigenisation shortens the period in which controls bind and costs the US a market, but it cannot push the ratio below what selling chips would produce. If cheap domestic chips would let China out-buy its open-access appetite, the model understates the downside of controls.
    • The compute stock turns over at a constant rate. At the default rate more than half of installed compute is under a year old, so a cut-off restores most of the gap within two years. If growth slows, old chips matter longer and a late cut-off recovers less. China may also stockpile if it expects one.
    • The lead depends on the compute ratio alone. Algorithms, talent and how concentrated each country's compute is are held at today's levels.
    • One window, one conversion curve. Nothing after the window is modelled, and a lead is either converted or not.
    • China's responses are response functions, not optimised strategies. Leakage, the captive share, the mandate and Beijing's credence stand in for choices a fuller game would derive. Retaliation outside semiconductors is not included.
    • Third-country buyers switching to Chinese suppliers appear only as a cost, not as extra demand that speeds China's industry.
    • The range sample draws assumptions independently and uniformly. It describes the ranges, not a belief about them.

    The model on one page

    1 · Policy to compute

    foreign = (1 − chips) + chips · leak · (1 − enforce)

    captive = max(mandate, 1 − foreign)

    domestic = min(q · e, captive · appetite)

    flow = domestic + min(foreign · appetite, appetite − domestic)

    c′ = (1 − turnover) · c + turnover · flow   R = 1 / c

    drive = captive · (1 − choke · tools)

    q′ = q · (1 + growth · drive) / (1 + usGrowth)

    e′ = e + catchup · drive · (1 − e)

    c: China's compute stock over the US's. q: Chinese chip units over US unit purchases. e: Chinese chip performance over the US frontier chip.

    2 · Compute to lead

    lead = lead₀ + β · log₁₀(R / R₀)

    3 · Lead to outcomes

    P(window opens in year t) = pD · lognormal(t; median)

    P(US clearly ahead) = E[ x³ / (x³ + τ³) ], x = lead + noise > 0

    P(China clearly ahead): the same with −x. Otherwise contested.

    score = Σ P(case) · (1 − qcase) · vcase / 100

    4 · Value of A over B

    strategic = stakes · Σt P(window in t) · dt · (scoreA − scoreB)

    lead value = Σt dt gt · (blead − bopen) · (leadA − leadB)

    sales = Σt dt gt · surplus · market · Δ(foreign chips bought / appetite)

    third countries = −Σt dt gt · third · Δ(enforce · chips)

    war = −war cost · Δ[ denial · w₀ + denial at window · w₁ · κ · pD ]

    d: discount factor. g: growth of yearly flows. denial: mean of the chip and tool levers.
